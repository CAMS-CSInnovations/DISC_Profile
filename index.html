How do you work best on a team?

DISC is a shared language for how people approach tasks and each other. Learn the four styles, find your own, and learn how each teammate wants to be treated.

What is DISC?

DISC is a model of behavior that comes from psychologist William Moulton Marston, who described it in 1928. It sorts how people tend to act into four styles: Dominance, Influence, Steadiness, and Conscientiousness. Over a million people a year use DISC-based assessments at work, and many companies ask new employees to take one.

DISC measures your preferences: how fast you like to move, what you pay attention to, what motivates you, what stresses you out, and how you handle conflict. It doesn't measure intelligence, talent, or whether you'll succeed. Everyone is a blend of all four styles; most people lean strongly toward one or two.

Not everyone works the way you do

It's natural to communicate the way you'd like others to communicate with you. But your teammates may want something different. A high D who likes blunt, fast feedback can overwhelm a high S who needs time and a calm tone. A high C who wants every detail can lose a high I who just wants the big picture.

DISC gives your team a common language for these differences, so you can notice how each person works best and adjust how you communicate with them.

The two questions behind DISC

Every style comes from where you land on two questions.

Pace: outgoing or reserved?
Do you tend to speak up, act quickly, and take risks (D and I)? Or do you think first, move carefully, and prefer a steady pace (S and C)?
Focus: tasks or people?
Do you naturally focus on the work, the logic, and questioning ideas (D and C)? Or on the people, the relationships, and accepting others (I and S)?

How each style reacts to conflict

Under pressure, D and I styles tend to vent (fight). S and C styles tend to suppress (flight). Neither one solves the problem on its own. Knowing your reaction lets you choose a better one.

    What DISC does for your team

    • Raises self-awarenessBetter teamwork starts with knowing yourself: how you react to conflict, what motivates you, what stresses you out, and how you solve problems.
    • Improves teamwork and communicationA high D wants the bottom line. A high C wants the details. A high I wants to talk it out. A high S wants time to think. Sending the same message four different ways isn't fake; it's good communication.
    • Makes conflict productiveMost team friction is a style clash, not bad intentions. Naming the difference makes it easier to fix and keeps the project moving.
    • Makes leaders more effectivePMs and team leads who know their teammates' styles can assign cards, give feedback, and run stand-ups in ways that actually work for each person.
    • Balances your sprint boardSomeone who pushes work to Done, someone who pitches the idea, someone who keeps everyone unstuck, and someone who catches the bug before Deploy. A team of all one style has a predictable blind spot.

    Keep in mind

    There is no best style. Every style is valuable to the process. Great leaders, engineers, and designers come from all four.

    You are all four. One style may lead, but the goal is learning to use the style a situation needs: taking advantage of your strengths and working around your weaknesses.

    Be honest. Answer based on how you usually act, not how you think you should. Otherwise your results won't mean anything.

    It's not an excuse. "I'm a high D, so I'm just blunt" doesn't get you out of being kind. Knowing your style means you can adjust it when the team needs you to.
